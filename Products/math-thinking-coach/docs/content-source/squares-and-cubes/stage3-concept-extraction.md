# Stage 3 — Concept Extraction: Squares and Cubes

## Section 1 — Square numbers and their properties
- **Perfect square**: a number expressible as n × n for a natural number n. *(Ch.1 §1.1)*
- **Unit's-digit test**: a perfect square can only end in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9. A number ending in 2, 3, 7 or 8 can never be a perfect square — but ending in an allowed digit does not guarantee it *is* one. *(Ch.1 §1.1 / prior Ch.5 §5.2)*
- **Odd-number-sum test**: every perfect square n² is the sum of the first n consecutive odd numbers starting from 1; a number that cannot be fully reduced to 0 by successively subtracting 1, 3, 5, ... is not a perfect square. *(Ch.1 §1.1 / prior Ch.5 §5.3)*
- **Numbers between consecutive squares**: there are exactly 2n non-square numbers strictly between n² and (n+1)². *(prior Ch.5 §5.3)*
- **Pythagorean triplets**: for any natural number m > 1, the numbers 2m, m² − 1, m² + 1 form a Pythagorean triplet (their squares satisfy a² + b² = c²). Not every triplet arises this way, but this form always produces one. *(prior Ch.5 §5.4.2)*

## Section 2 — Finding square roots
- **Square root**: the inverse operation of squaring; if y = x², x is a square root of y. This course uses only the positive square root, denoted √. *(Ch.1 §1.1)*
- **Repeated-subtraction method**: successively subtract consecutive odd numbers (1, 3, 5, ...) from a perfect square until reaching exactly 0; the count of subtractions is the square root. *(Ch.1 §1.1 / prior Ch.5 §5.5.2)*
- **Prime-factorisation method**: write the number as a product of primes, pair up identical factors, and multiply one factor from each pair to get the square root. If any prime factor cannot be paired, the number is not a perfect square. *(Ch.1 §1.1 / prior Ch.5 §5.5.3)*
- **Smallest multiplier/divisor**: to make a non-square number a perfect square, identify the unpaired prime factor(s) in its factorisation and either multiply by the missing partner(s) or divide out the unpaired factor(s). *(prior Ch.5 §5.5.3, Examples 6–8)*
- **Estimation**: a non-perfect-square's root can be bracketed between two consecutive whole numbers by comparing it to nearby known squares, then narrowed further. *(Ch.1 §1.1)*
- **Applications**: area of a square → side length; Pythagoras' theorem (hypotenuse/leg) computations that reduce to a square-root calculation. *(prior Ch.5 §5.5, Figs 5.1–5.2)*

## Section 3 — Cube numbers and their properties
- **Perfect cube**: a number expressible as n × n × n for a natural number n. *(Ch.1 §1.2)*
- **Prime-factorisation test**: a number is a perfect cube only if every prime factor in its factorisation appears in a group of exactly three (a "triple"); an incomplete triple means the number is not a perfect cube. *(Ch.1 §1.2 / prior Ch.6 §6.2)*
- **Smallest multiplier/divisor**: to complete an incomplete triple, either multiply by the missing factor(s) or divide out the excess/unpaired factor(s). *(prior Ch.6 §6.2, Examples 2–5)*
- **Hardy–Ramanujan number**: 1729 is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways (1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³) — a concrete, checkable numeric fact rather than an abstract property. *(Ch.1 §1.2)*

## Section 4 — Finding cube roots
- **Cube root**: the inverse operation of cubing; if y = x³, x is the cube root of y, denoted ∛. *(Ch.1 §1.2)*
- **Prime-factorisation method**: write the number as a product of primes, group identical factors into triples, and multiply one factor from each triple to get the cube root. *(Ch.1 §1.2 / prior Ch.6 §6.3.1)*
- **Applications**: volume of a cube → edge length, by the same prime-factorisation method. *(prior Ch.6 §6.3.1, Example 6)*
